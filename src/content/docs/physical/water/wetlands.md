A **wetland** is an area where the land is covered by water, or the soil is saturated, for all or part of the year. This includes marshes, swamps, bogs, fens, floodplains and mangrove forests. Wetlands are the transition between land and water, and they host plants and animals adapted to wet conditions. They cover a small fraction of the planet but are among its most productive ecosystems.

## Types of wetland

- **Marshes** are dominated by grasses, reeds and other soft-stemmed plants and may be fresh or salty.
- **Swamps** are dominated by trees, such as those in the Amazon floodplain and the cypress swamps of the southeastern United States.
- **Bogs** are acidic wetlands fed mainly by rain, where partly decayed plants build up as **peat**.
- **Fens** are similar to bogs but are fed by groundwater and are less acidic.
- **Mangroves** are tidal forests along tropical coasts; see [mangroves](../../biomes/mangroves/).

## Why wetlands matter

Wetlands act like sponges, soaking up floodwater and releasing it slowly, which reduces flood damage downstream. They filter pollutants, recharge [groundwater](../groundwater/) and shelter fish nurseries and migrating birds. Peatlands store enormous amounts of carbon, so draining them releases greenhouse gases; see [climate change](../../climate/climate-change/).

## Notable wetlands

The Pantanal in [Brazil](../../../world/south-america/brazil/) is one of the largest tropical wetlands, and the Sudd in South Sudan lies along the White Nile. Many [deltas](../deltas/), such as the Nile and the Mississippi, contain extensive wetlands, and [estuaries](../estuaries/) hold salt marshes. The [Amazon rainforest](../../../places/amazon-rainforest/) includes large seasonally flooded forests.

## Loss and protection

Large areas have been drained for farming and building, and some estimates suggest that a large share of the world's wetlands have disappeared since 1900. International agreements, such as the Ramsar Convention, now aim to protect important sites.
