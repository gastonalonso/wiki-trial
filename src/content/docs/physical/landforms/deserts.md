A **desert** is a region that receives very little precipitation, generally less than about 250 millimetres a year. Deserts cover roughly a third of the land surface and are found on every continent. Despite the common image, many deserts are not hot and sandy: the largest on Earth are the polar deserts of Antarctica and the Arctic.

## Why deserts form

Several causes produce dry land. Around 30 degrees north and south of the equator, air sinks and warms, suppressing cloud and rain; the [Sahara](../../../places/sahara/) and the Australian deserts lie in these belts. Deserts also form in the **rain shadow** of [mountains](../mountains/), where moist air has already dropped its water on the far side. Others are far from the sea, or lie along cold ocean currents, as the Atacama in [Chile](../../../world/south-america/chile/) does. See [climate zones](../../climate/climate-zones/) for the wider pattern.

## Types of desert

- **Hot deserts**, such as the Sahara and the Arabian Desert, have high daytime temperatures.
- **Cold deserts**, such as the Gobi in [Mongolia](../../../world/asia/mongolia/) and China, have freezing winters.
- **Polar deserts** are covered by ice and receive very little snowfall.
- **Coastal deserts** occur where cold currents cool the air, such as the Namib and the Atacama.

## Landscape and life

Only a minority of desert surface is sand. Much is bare rock, gravel plains or dry salt flats. Wind builds dunes, and rare flash floods cut dry riverbeds called wadis. Plants and animals survive by storing water, being active at night or lying dormant for years. People have crossed deserts for millennia, relying on oases, where groundwater reaches the surface. See [groundwater](../../water/groundwater/).

:::note
Temperatures in hot deserts can swing by more than 30 degrees Celsius between day and night, because the dry air holds little heat.
:::
