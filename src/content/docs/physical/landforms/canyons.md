A **canyon** is a deep valley with steep, often vertical sides, usually cut into rock by a river. Canyons are most common in dry regions, where the rock stays firm enough to hold a steep face instead of being rounded off by plant roots and heavy rain. A very narrow canyon with high walls is sometimes called a **gorge**, and a slot canyon may be only a metre or two wide.

## How canyons form

Canyon formation needs two things: a river with enough energy to cut downward, and land that is rising or has a hard rock layer to resist widening. As a plateau is uplifted, the river cuts down to keep its level, slicing through layer after layer of rock. Side streams, rockfalls and flash floods slowly widen the walls, giving many canyons a stepped profile where hard layers form cliffs and soft layers form slopes. The walls therefore act as a record of geological history, with older rock at the bottom. See [plateaus](../plateaus/) and [valleys](../valleys/) for related landforms.

## Famous canyons

- The [Grand Canyon](../../../places/grand-canyon/) in Arizona, cut by the Colorado River, is about 450 kilometres long and up to roughly 1.8 kilometres deep.
- The Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon in Tibet is often cited as the deepest in the world.
- Canyons also occur under the sea: submarine canyons cut the continental slopes and carry sediment to the deep ocean floor.

Figures for depth and length vary depending on where the rim and the bottom are measured, so published values differ between sources.[^1]

## Canyons and people

Canyons funnel rivers and can be dammed for water storage and hydropower. They also attract tourism, hiking and climbing, and they have sheltered human communities for thousands of years. Flash floods are a serious risk: rain far upstream can send a wall of water through a narrow canyon with little warning.
