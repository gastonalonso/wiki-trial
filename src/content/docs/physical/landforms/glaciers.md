A **glacier** is a large mass of ice that forms on land from compacted snow and slowly flows under its own weight. Glaciers hold about two thirds of the world's fresh water. Most of that ice is in the two great ice sheets of [Antarctica](../../../world/antarctica/) and Greenland, but thousands of smaller glaciers sit in mountain ranges on every continent except Australia.

## How glaciers form and move

Where more snow falls in winter than melts in summer, layers build up. Over years the weight squeezes out air and turns snow into dense ice. Once the ice is thick enough, it begins to flow downhill or outward, usually at a rate of a few centimetres to several metres a day. The upper part gains mass in the **accumulation zone**, and the lower part loses it by melting and calving in the **ablation zone**. When the ice front reaches the sea, chunks break off as icebergs.

## Types of glacier

- **Ice sheets** cover whole regions and bury the land beneath them.
- **Valley glaciers** flow down mountain valleys.
- **Piedmont glaciers** spread out where a valley glacier reaches flat land.
- **Ice shelves** are floating platforms of ice attached to the coast.

## How glaciers shape land

Glaciers are powerful agents of erosion. They scrape bedrock, carve U-shaped [valleys](../valleys/), gouge out basins that become [lakes](../../water/lakes/), and leave behind ridges and mounds of debris called moraines. When glaciers melt back, rivers carry the meltwater downstream, so many large rivers depend on seasonal ice melt. Glaciers in many regions are retreating as the climate warms; see [climate change](../../climate/climate-change/).

:::caution
Glacial melt contributes to sea-level rise. Ice already floating, such as sea ice, does not add much, but melting land ice does.
:::
