A **valley** is a long, low area of land between hills or mountains, usually with a river or stream running along the bottom. Valleys are among the most common landforms and have long been centres of settlement, because they offer water, flat ground and fertile soil. Many of the world's early civilisations grew in river valleys such as those of the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, and the Indus.

## River valleys

Most valleys are cut by running water. Near the source of a river, where the slope is steep, the stream cuts downward and produces a narrow **V-shaped valley**. Farther downstream, the river swings from side to side, widening the floor into a flat **floodplain** that is built up by layers of silt from repeated floods. The same processes at larger scale create [canyons](../canyons/). See [rivers](../../water/rivers/) for how channels move and change.

## Glacial valleys

Glaciers shape valleys differently. A moving mass of ice is thick and heavy, and it scours the sides and floor, leaving a broad **U-shaped valley** with steep walls and a flat bottom. When the ice melts, small tributary valleys may be left hanging high above the main one, often ending in waterfalls. Where a glacial valley meets the sea and floods, it becomes a **fjord**, a feature typical of [Norway](../../../world/europe/norway/) and of Chile. Learn more on the [glaciers](../glaciers/) page.

## Other kinds of valley

- **Rift valleys** form where blocks of crust drop between parallel faults, as in East Africa and in the valley holding the [Dead Sea](../../../places/dead-sea/).
- **Dry valleys** have no permanent stream, either because the climate has changed or because water flows underground in limestone.
- **Hanging valleys** perch above a larger valley, left by glacial erosion.

:::note
Not every valley holds a river. Some of the driest places on Earth, including parts of Antarctica, have valleys cut entirely by ice or by water that has long since gone.
:::
