title: Ocean currents

An **ocean current** is a persistent, directed movement of seawater. Currents carry heat from the tropics toward the poles and return cold water toward the equator, and so they strongly influence the climate of nearby coasts. They also steer marine life, shipping routes and drifting debris.

## Surface currents

Surface currents are driven mainly by prevailing winds, the trade winds and westerlies, and are deflected by Earth's rotation (the **Coriolis effect**) and by the shape of the continents. This produces large circular systems called **gyres**: clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern. Five major gyres exist, in the North and South Atlantic, North and South Pacific, and Indian Ocean.

Well-known surface currents include:

- **Gulf Stream**: a warm, fast current from the Gulf of Mexico across the North Atlantic. It helps keep northwestern Europe, including [Norway](../../../world/europe/norway/) and [Iceland](../../../world/europe/iceland/), milder than other places at the same latitude.
- **Humboldt (Peru) Current**: a cold current along the west coast of South America that supports major fisheries and helps create the dry coast of [Chile](../../../world/south-america/chile/) and [Peru](../../../world/south-america/peru/).
- **Kuroshio**: a warm current off the east coast of [Japan](../../../world/asia/japan/).
- **Antarctic Circumpolar Current**: the largest current by volume, flowing eastward around Antarctica.

## Deep circulation

Below the surface, water moves because of differences in density, which depends on temperature and salinity. Cold, salty water sinks near Greenland and Antarctica and spreads slowly along the ocean floor. This **thermohaline circulation**, sometimes called the global conveyor belt, links all the [oceans](../../water/oceans/) and takes roughly a thousand years to complete a cycle.

:::tip[Did you know?]
Currents are measured in sverdrups, a unit equal to one million cubic metres of water per second. The Gulf Stream carries tens of sverdrups, far more than all the world's rivers combined.
:::

## Climate links

Currents shape coastal temperature and rainfall, change during [El Niño](../el-nino/), and may be affected by melting ice in a warming world. See [climate change](../climate-change/).
