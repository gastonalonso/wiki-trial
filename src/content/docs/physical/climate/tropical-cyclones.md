A **tropical cyclone** is a large, rotating storm system with a low-pressure centre, strong winds and heavy rain, which forms over warm tropical oceans. The same type of storm has different names in different parts of the world.

*Photo: [Alexander Gerst](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Staring_Down_Hurricane_Florence.jpg), Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

## How they form

Tropical cyclones need sea surface temperatures of roughly 26.5 degrees Celsius or higher, moist air, and little change in wind speed with height. Warm water evaporates and rises, releasing heat as clouds form, which powers the storm. Earth's rotation makes the system spin, counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern. Because this spin is weak at the equator, these storms rarely form within about 5 degrees of it.

A storm is usually called a **tropical storm** once its sustained winds pass about 63 kilometres per hour, and a hurricane, typhoon or cyclone once they reach about 119 kilometres per hour. The calm **eye** at the centre is surrounded by the **eyewall**, where the strongest winds and rain occur.

## Regional names

| Region | Name | Typical season |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North Atlantic, northeast Pacific | Hurricane | June to November |
| Northwest Pacific | Typhoon | May to November, peak in late summer |
| North Indian Ocean | Cyclone | April to June and October to December |
| South Indian Ocean, southwest Pacific | Cyclone | November to April |

## Effects

The greatest dangers are often not the wind. A **storm surge**, a rise in sea level pushed ashore by the storm, causes much of the loss of life on low [coastlines](../../landforms/coastlines/) and in [deltas](../../water/deltas/). Heavy rain brings inland floods. Storms weaken over land or cooler water, so island nations and coasts of [Japan](../../../world/asia/japan/), [Mexico](../../../world/north-america/mexico/) and the [United States](../../../world/north-america/united-states/) are most exposed.

:::danger
Storm surge and flooding are the leading causes of death in tropical cyclones. Follow official evacuation orders rather than judging risk by wind strength alone.
:::
