The **taiga**, also called **boreal forest**, is a belt of coniferous forest that circles the Northern Hemisphere south of the Arctic. The word is Russian. It is the largest land biome on Earth, stretching across Alaska, Canada, Scandinavia and Russia, and its Siberian section alone covers millions of square kilometres.

## Climate

Winters are long and severe, with average temperatures often below minus 20 degrees Celsius in the coldest regions, while summers are short and mild. Siberia has some of the largest seasonal ranges on Earth. Annual precipitation is modest, about 300 to 900 millimetres, and much of it falls as snow. This matches the continental group D in the [climate zones](../../climate/climate-zones/).

## Vegetation and soils

Spruce, fir, pine and larch dominate, with birch and aspen in places. The conical shape and flexible branches of conifers shed snow, and their needles resist freezing and water loss. Soils are acidic and thin, and the cold slows decay, so a layer of needles builds up. In the north, **permafrost**, ground frozen year-round, lies beneath much of the forest and holds large stores of carbon.

## Wildlife and resources

Moose, brown bears, wolves, lynx, reindeer (caribou) and many nesting birds live in the taiga, and many species migrate north or south with the seasons. Fire and insect outbreaks renew the forest naturally. Logging, oil and gas, and mining are the main human uses, and the forest is an important store of carbon. Around [Lake Baikal](../../../places/lake-baikal/) in Siberia, the taiga surrounds the world's deepest lake.

:::caution
Thawing permafrost and larger wildfires in a warming climate can release stored carbon and turn the taiga from a carbon sink into a source. See [climate change](../../climate/climate-change/).
:::
