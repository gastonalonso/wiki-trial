**New Zealand**, known in Māori as Aotearoa, lies in the south-west Pacific about 1,500 kilometres east of Australia. It consists of two main islands, the North Island and the South Island, and many smaller ones. It was among the last substantial land areas to be settled by people.

## Geography

New Zealand lies on the boundary between the Pacific and Australian plates, and its landscape reflects that. The **Southern Alps** run down the South Island and hold glaciers such as the Franz Josef and Fox ([mountains](../../../physical/landforms/mountains/), [glaciers](../../../physical/landforms/glaciers/)). The deeply cut fjords of Fiordland are carved by ice ([coastlines](../../../physical/landforms/coastlines/)). The North Island has an active volcanic zone, including Ruapehu and the Taupō caldera, plus hot springs and geysers ([volcanoes](../../../physical/landforms/volcanoes/)).

The South Island's east side has the broad Canterbury Plains, and Lake Taupō in the North Island is the largest lake in the country ([lakes](../../../physical/water/lakes/)). Earthquakes are common, and major ones have struck Christchurch and Wellington.

## Climate and biomes

The climate is mild and temperate and strongly shaped by the sea. The west coast of the South Island is very wet, since moist westerly winds drop rain against the Alps, while the eastern side is much drier. Native forests of podocarps and southern beech survive in many areas ([temperate forests](../../../physical/biomes/temperate-forests/)), though much of the lowland was cleared for farming, and sheep and dairy cattle are central to the economy.

:::tip[Did you know?]
The islands had no native land mammals apart from bats, and several flightless birds, including the kiwi, evolved there.
:::
