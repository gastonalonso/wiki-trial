The **Aral Sea** is a lake in Central Asia, between Kazakhstan in the north and Uzbekistan in the south. Until the 1960s it was the fourth largest lake in the world by area. Since then it has lost most of its water, in one of the best-known environmental disasters of the twentieth century.

## Why it shrank

Two rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, fed the lake. In the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted much of their water into canals to irrigate cotton and other crops in the desert. With less water arriving and strong evaporation in the dry climate, the lake level fell and its shoreline moved back by many kilometres. By the early 2000s the lake had split into several parts.

## Consequences

- Fishing towns that once stood on the shore were left far from the water, and the fishing industry collapsed.
- The salt content of the remaining water rose, killing most fish species.
- The exposed lake bed, covered in salt and agricultural chemicals, produced dust storms that affected health in the surrounding region.
- The local climate became drier, with hotter summers and colder winters.

## The northern Aral

In the north, Kazakhstan built the Kokaral Dam, completed in 2005, to hold the Syr Darya's water in the smaller northern part. The water level there rose, salinity fell and fish returned, so fishing has partly recovered. The southern part, in Uzbekistan, has continued to shrink, and a new desert, the Aralkum, has formed on the former lake bed.

## Lessons

The Aral Sea shows how diverting rivers affects a closed [lake](../../physical/water/lakes/) basin. It is a reminder that water use upstream can reshape landscapes, economies and health far downstream.
