Tundra

Tundra is a treeless biome found around the Arctic Ocean and on high mountains above the tree line. The name comes from a Sámi word meaning treeless plain. Arctic tundra stretches across northern Canada, Alaska, Greenland, Scandinavia and Siberia, and alpine tundra occurs on mountains such as the Himalayas and Andes.

Photo: Artem Abdukakharov, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Climate and permafrost

Section titled “Climate and permafrost”

Winters are long, dark and extremely cold, and the growing season lasts only 50 to 60 days. Precipitation is low, often under 250 millimetres a year, so the tundra is technically a cold desert, yet it is often wet because the cold limits evaporation. Beneath the surface lies permafrost. Only the top layer thaws in summer, which keeps water at the surface and forms pools and bogs. See climate zones for the polar group.

Life on the tundra

Section titled “Life on the tundra”

Plants stay close to the ground to escape wind and cold: mosses, lichens, sedges, dwarf willows and flowering cushions. Animals include caribou, musk oxen, Arctic foxes, snowy owls, lemmings and polar bears on the coast. Many birds arrive in summer to breed and leave before winter.

Change

Section titled “Change”

The Arctic is warming faster than the global average. Thawing permafrost releases carbon dioxide and methane, damages roads and buildings, and shifts the tree line northward as the taiga advances. Melting ice also affects the glaciers and ice sheets of the far north. Antarctica has little tundra, as most of it is ice; see Antarctica.
